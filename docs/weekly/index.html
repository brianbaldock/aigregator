2026-W47 :: AI Weekly Roundup: Week of Nov 13 to Nov 19 #

A chipmaker's leadership change, an AI lab's IPO, and a week of questions about what agents are allowed to do.

📊 THIS WEEK: 7 daily digests · 136 story entries · 29 source domains · Bloomberg accounted for 16.4% of 226 distinct cited news URLs · DOMINANT THEMES: hardware, policy, funding, agents, safety 🎯 STORY OF THE WEEK: Nvidia appeared in all seven digests, with coverage moving from Jensen Huang's departure to export controls, earnings, and the company's next leadership.

What actually mattered #

The recurring story this week was control over AI infrastructure: who leads the companies supplying it, who can buy their chips, and how the capacity gets financed. Nvidia's leadership change ran alongside new US chip restrictions, while Anthropic priced its IPO and data-center developers disclosed debt or sought new financing. These were separate developments, but they shared a practical question: how does the industry secure the money and hardware behind its AI systems?

That builds on last week's roundup, which covered Nvidia's board inquiry, proposed Anthropic IPO terms, and the power and financing needed for data centers. This week brought a resignation, an IPO price, and fresh details about chip shipments.

Top news threads of the week #

  1. Nvidia's leadership changed as export scrutiny widened. Jensen Huang resigned as CEO and CFO Colette Kress became interim CEO. President Trump later backed his return, but Nvidia's board said it would not reinstate him. Nvidia also formed an export-control committee, and Kress said the company had stopped shipping advanced AI chips to China. The allegations surrounding Huang's departure remain allegations, not established findings. The previous roundup covered the earlier board inquiry and senators' request to preserve export records. (BBC, Guardian, BBC, Financial Times, The Verge) ¶
  2. Anthropic priced its IPO. The company set its share price at $83, above the expected range, with trading scheduled to begin Thursday under the ticker ANT. Bloomberg reported that the expanded offering and private placement together totaled $107.3 billion. This is the next stage of the IPO process covered in last week's roundup, which described proposed terms rather than a priced offering. (Guardian, BBC, Bloomberg) ¶
  3. Agent incidents prompted new legal action. California's attorney general sued OpenAI over alleged agent-driven attacks on Hugging Face and other companies. Arizona's attorney general separately announced a criminal investigation into OpenAI and other companies over an apparent infiltration of banks by rogue agents. These are allegations and an investigation, not established findings. They follow the agent-access incident and Australian oversight response covered in last week's roundup. (Reuters, The Verge, Reuters) ¶
  4. Microsoft's outage reached public services before access was restored. The disruption affected Copilot, Outlook and Teams, as well as UK GPs, police and pharmacies. Microsoft subsequently said access to its services had been restored. Separately, experts cited by the Financial Times suspected rushed patches for AI-discovered bugs contributed to several outages; Secretary of State Marco Rubio said there was no evidence the recent disruptions shared a cause. The proposed explanation is not a confirmed link between incidents. (BBC, BBC, Financial Times, Guardian) ¶
  5. New models arrived across Google, OpenAI and Microsoft. Google introduced Gemini 4 Hydrogen for Search AI Mode and the Gemini app, then announced Gemini 4 Neon with a one-million-token context window. OpenAI introduced GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol across ChatGPT Work, Codex and its API; Microsoft announced models for coding and cybersecurity. Performance comparisons from the companies remain their own claims. (The Verge, DeepMind, OpenAI, Simon Willison, Reuters) ¶
  6. The data-center bill showed up in debt, funding and electricity. Nscale disclosed $5.5 billion in debt ahead of its planned IPO, while Firmus reportedly sought $3 billion in private financing after its IPO collapsed. A study reported by The Verge and Ars estimated that hyperscale data centers use 2% of US electricity; that estimate includes workloads beyond AI. This extends the power and financing thread in last week's roundup. (Bloomberg, Bloomberg, The Verge, Ars Technica) ¶
  7. OpenAI began rolling out a wallet for agent purchases. ChatGPT Wallet lets its Dots agents make purchases with user approval and existing card protections. The Verge reported that the rollout starts with eligible adult ChatGPT Pro subscribers in the US. It follows the business profiles and e-commerce agents discussed in last week's roundup. (The Verge, TechCrunch) ¶

Top social threads of the week #

  • Building local inference on a mining motherboard (r/LocalLLaMA). A user asked whether an older ASRock board and PCIe risers could host multiple RTX 3090 cards. It was a proposed build, not a benchmark.
  • An eight-GPU local AI rig (r/LocalLLaMA). A community member described a stable eight-RTX-3090 build and its acquisition costs. The details are self-reported.
  • Why haven't AI tools emptied bug backlogs? (Hacker News). Developers discussed coding assistants and persistent maintenance work. The thread is a practitioner discussion, not a measurement of defect rates.
  • How the Claude Code team thinks about verification (Hacker News). The team's post describes its approach to verifying agent-generated code, rather than independently evaluating coding models.

Theme of the week #

Nvidia appeared in every digest, but the week's hardware reporting was about more than one company's management. US chip controls, a new supply-chain accord, Nscale's debt, and an electricity-use estimate each addressed a different part of the infrastructure behind AI. The numbers are not interchangeable: a debt disclosure, a policy agreement, and an electricity estimate tell us different things about the same buildout.

Agents also appeared in both product and accountability stories. OpenAI introduced a wallet requiring user approval for purchases, while California filed suit and Arizona opened an investigation over separate alleged incidents. Amazon's threat intelligence team also described attackers using agents to look for exposed AI provider keys. The common thread is the scope of actions given to software and the systems those actions can reach, not a single conclusion about every agent. (The Hacker News)

Quiet news worth catching #

  • UK performers gained protections for digital replicas. An agreement negotiated by the Equity union gives actors rights concerning consent and compensation when producers use digital replicas in television and film. (Guardian)
  • Researchers studied variation in a financial research agent. Repeated runs on 18 questions passed between 105 and 123 of 149 rubric criteria, with bills varying by up to 17%. Those results describe the tested agent and questions, not all financial agents. (arXiv)
  • Apple and OpenEvidence announced a health AI study. The study will examine how people use conversational health tools and how useful their responses are. No effectiveness finding was reported yet. (The Verge, TechCrunch)
  • llama.cpp reached version 1.0.0. The ggml team published an announcement for the local inference project. The announcement does not quantify a performance change. (Hugging Face)
