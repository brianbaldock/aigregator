2026-W48 :: AI Weekly Roundup: Week of Nov 20 to Nov 26 #

A chipmaker's return, an unverified mathematics claim, and the practical limits on what AI agents can do.

📊 THIS WEEK: 7 daily digests · 137 story entries · 25 source domains · Bloomberg accounted for 17.9% of 212 distinct URLs cited in story sections 🎯 STORY OF THE WEEK: Nvidia appeared in all seven digests, with coverage spanning its leadership, export controls and investigations.

What actually mattered #

The recurring story this week was control over AI infrastructure: who runs the companies supplying it, where their chips can go, and how much the hardware costs. Nvidia's leadership reversal and export scrutiny ran alongside reports of higher server prices and another proposed data-center project, this one in space.

That continues a thread from last week's roundup, which covered Jensen Huang's departure, Anthropic's IPO pricing and data-center financing. This week brought Huang's reinstatement and a close below Anthropic's IPO price.

Top news threads of the week #

  1. Nvidia reinstated Huang as chip scrutiny continued. Huang returned as CEO and, according to Bloomberg, apologized to staff and acknowledged violating the company's code of conduct. He is due to face congressional questioning in December. Separately, the US plans to blacklist Megaspeed over allegations that it routed AI chips to China, and the Financial Times reports Nvidia has halted shipments to Southeast Asia. The allegations and investigations are not findings of wrongdoing. Last week covered Huang's resignation and the earlier export-control response. (Nvidia, Bloomberg, Financial Times, BBC, Financial Times) ¶
  2. Anthropic shares closed below their IPO price. Ongoing since Nov 19: after closing their first trading day at $97, shares fell 13% and later closed below the $83 IPO price. The price and planned listing were covered in last week's roundup. (Guardian, Financial Times, Financial Times, TechCrunch) ¶
  3. A claimed Claude-assisted proof drew mathematical scrutiny. Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge says Claude helped him find a proof of the Riemann hypothesis. The Guardian reported that two groups had checked the claim. The proof has not been independently established, and the claim about Claude's contribution is a separate question from whether the argument is correct. (Bloomberg, AP, Guardian) ¶
  4. Meta's Muse launched in the UK while shopping access remained contested. Meta rolled out the agent for tasks including shopping and travel booking. Amazon and Meta have also reportedly discussed allowing Muse to shop on Amazon, where it is currently blocked. Google, meanwhile, added computer use to Gemini on macOS, while an Apple job listing described a cross-app agent in development, not a released product. The shopping thread follows the agent wallet covered in last week's roundup. (Guardian, The Verge, TechCrunch, The Verge, TechCrunch) ¶
  5. Tidal set a ban on fully AI-generated music. The streaming service says it plans to remove more than 27 million fully AI-generated tracks and that 43% of uploads in the previous 90 days were entirely AI-made. Those figures are Tidal's own counts. (Guardian, The Verge, Ars Technica) ¶
  6. States acted on chatbot data and products. Forty-two state attorneys general sought to block the proposed sale of Character.AI user data in bankruptcy, saying private chats from more than 21 million US accounts could be auctioned. New York enacted a five-year ban on making or selling internet-connected chatbot toys. Oregon and Virginia separately sued over alleged inadequate safeguards against Grok sexual deepfakes; those claims remain allegations. (The Verge, TechCrunch, Guardian, The Verge) ¶
  7. SpaceX received approval for a proposed orbital data-center network. The authorization covers a proposed one-million-satellite constellation. Approval is not evidence that the satellites or computing infrastructure have been built. The infrastructure financing and power questions covered in last week's roundup remain distinct from this authorization. (BBC, The Verge, Ars Technica) ¶

Top social and community threads #

  • Google workers' school AI petition (shared on Hacker News). The report says workers asked Google not to penalize schools that decline AI tools.
  • OpenRouter's account of data-processing incidents (shared on Hacker News). OpenRouter describes incidents at inference providers; it is the operator's account, alongside reporting that three providers were removed after researchers reported prompt theft. (The Hacker News)
  • An Anthropic distillation allegation (Hacker News). A founder says Anthropic accused their company of model distillation and asks what evidence could rebut it. The allegation and response are unverified.
  • A Lean mathematics repository (shared on Hacker News). The project presents a shorter formalization of a generalized Riemann hypothesis verification associated with Anthropic work. That repository claim is not an independently reviewed resolution of the Riemann hypothesis.

Theme of the week #

Nvidia appeared in all seven digests, but the infrastructure story extended beyond its leadership. The Financial Times reported that server suppliers sought price increases of up to 45% after agreements were signed; Bloomberg reported that Amazon agreed to acquire much of Decart AI for $7.5 billion. The latter is a reported agreement, not a reported completed acquisition. Server prices, acquisition terms and chip controls are different pieces of the same hardware and compute picture. (Financial Times, Bloomberg)

The other recurring question was what happens when AI systems reach beyond a chat window. Muse's shopping access, Gemini's computer use and OpenRouter's provider incidents concern different systems, but all turn attention to the services and data those systems touch. Product access, permissions and data handling are not the same problem, and none of these reports settles the others.

Quiet news worth catching #

  • A Dutch watchdog warned against buying AI smart glasses. Tests it cited found glasses misread medicine instructions and, in one case, falsely claimed to have called an ambulance. (BBC)
  • Australia's musicians rejected a proposed training carveout. The music industry's trade group opposed a reported exemption for musicians from AI scraping that would leave other creative sectors uncovered. No enacted policy was described. (Guardian)
  • OpenAI and Cisco introduced Shirudo. The companies describe the open-source Linux runtime as a way to protect systems while teams deploy patches. This is a vendor announcement, not an independent security assessment. (OpenAI)
  • The llama.cpp team published an AMD benchmark dashboard. It compares inference on Ryzen CPUs and Radeon GPUs; results should be read against the tested hardware and configurations. It follows the project's version 1.0 announcement in last week's roundup. (Hugging Face)
